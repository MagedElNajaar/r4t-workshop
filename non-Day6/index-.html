Day 6 case — fictional company
LaunchPad Pro · Product Rollout Case
Capstone case
Situation
A small product team is launching a workplace coordination platform. Product exists. Audience exists. But the internal work system is broken — no shared structure, no reporting, no automation, no communication process.
Business objective
Get the rollout to public launch within a set window. Track work clearly. Report to leadership without manual effort. Run without constant reminders. Produce professional, channel-ready communication outputs.
Team situation
5 people. 2 on the product. 3 on marketing, communication, and coordination. They share tools — but not a system. Work happens, but status is unclear, deadlines slip, and nobody outside the team can tell where the rollout actually stands.
Pressure
Launch in 6 weeks. Manager needs weekly visibility. Marketing outputs require brand review. Team lead is spending 40% of their week chasing updates and sending manual reminders.
End-of-day outcome
Trace how this team moved from that broken starting point to a structured, automated, communication-ready rollout system — one stage at a time, with one artifact at each step.
Without a system
Work happens in messages, emails, and memory. Nobody is sure what is done. The manager asks for an update and the team lead has to stop working to manually check five things before answering. Launch week arrives and two things were not finished because nobody caught the gap early enough.
This is not an unusual situation. It is the default state for many small teams working under pressure without a shared system.
With a structured workflow
Work is tracked in one place. Reports generate automatically. Reminders go out without anyone having to remember to send them. Communication outputs follow a consistent structure. The manager can see status without asking. The team lead spends their week on decisions, not updates.
That is the end state this case works toward. Today traces how the team built it — stage by stage.

Stage 1 — Structure and controlled tracking

5 Why Structure Came First

Before the team could report, automate, or draft anything reliably — the work had to become visible.

Reporting depends on having something to report from. Automation depends on consistent inputs. AI-supported drafting depends on accurate context. None of those things were possible when work lived in message threads and memory.

Before structure
Task status lives in messages that not everyone has seen
Nobody agrees on who owns what — it depends on who you ask
Deadlines are in calendar invites, chat threads, and personal notes — all separate
The manager asks for a status update and the team lead has to manually check four different places before answering
Blockers get noticed late because nobody has a clear view of what is stuck
After structure
Every task has one place it lives — with a status, an owner, and a deadline visible to the whole team
Ownership is named and recorded, not assumed
Progress is visible without anyone needing to ask
The manager can check status directly — the team lead is not the reporting layer
Blockers appear as soon as status is updated, not days later when a deadline passes
Structure is not the same as having more meetings or writing more notes.
Structure means work is visible, owned, and findable without asking anyone.

9 The Gap

The tracker solved the working view. It did not solve the leadership view.

A tracker contains every task, every owner, every note, and every status update. That is exactly what the team needs. A manager who just wants to know whether the rollout is on track does not need all of that — they need a filtered, decision-ready summary. Those are different things.

Tracker — working view
What it contains
Every task with its owner, status, due date, and next step
Detailed notes and reference links per item
30+ rows for a 6-week rollout
Useful for the team — requires reading to extract the picture
Not designed to answer "are we on track?" in 15 seconds
Dashboard — leadership view
What it shows
A summary of what is on track, at risk, or blocked right now
Items that need approval or escalation this week
What changed since the last update — without reading the whole tracker
Designed to answer "do I need to act on anything?" in under a minute
Built from the tracker — reads the same data, surfaces the right signals
The tracker is for people doing the work.
The dashboard is for people making decisions about the work.
Warning

A dashboard built on weak or inconsistent tracking gives false confidence. If the tracker fields are not kept up to date, the dashboard shows a picture that looks current but is not. The quality of Stage 2 is only as good as the data in Stage 1.

Stage 3 — Automation of repeated work

The tracker was built. The dashboard was live. But the team was still doing repeated coordination work by hand — sending reminders, routing approvals, nudging owners. Stage 3 removes that manual overhead by turning repeatable, rule-based actions into automatic ones.

13
13 Still Manual

Even with a tracker and a dashboard, the team lead was still doing repeated coordination work every day by hand.

Visibility improved. Reporting improved. But the follow-up work — the nudges, the reminders, the routing — still depended on one person remembering to do it. That is the gap Stage 3 closes.

1
Sending deadline reminders manually
The team lead checked the tracker each morning and sent personal messages to anyone whose deadline was approaching. This took 20–30 minutes every day and depended entirely on the team lead not forgetting.
2
Nudging owners for status updates
Tasks that stayed "in progress" for too long did not automatically flag. The team lead had to notice them, identify the owner, and send a follow-up. This happened multiple times per week per person.
3
Notifying reviewers when something was ready
When a deliverable changed status to "ready for review," the team lead had to notify the relevant reviewer. There was no automatic routing — it required checking the tracker and then sending a message.
4
Escalating overdue items to leadership
When something went overdue and needed a management decision, the team lead had to identify it, draft an escalation message, and send it. The dashboard showed it — but the action still required a human to trigger every time.
5
Following up on pending approvals
Approvals that had not been signed off after a set window required the team lead to personally follow up with the approver. No reminder fired automatically. Silence meant the approval could sit indefinitely.
Most of this work does not require fresh judgment every time.
It requires consistency. That is exactly what automation does better than a person.

19 The Review Loop

AI output is a starting point, not a final answer. The team runs a generate-compare-check-revise loop before anything moves forward.

The first output is a candidate. Comparing multiple candidates improves judgment. Checking against the brief prevents drift. Revising tightens expression without changing strategy. Tap each step to see how it works in the case.

1
Generate — run the prompt, produce three options
Tap to expand →
The team runs the locked prompt and asks for three draft variations — not one. Three options give the team something to compare rather than a single output to accept or reject. Each draft reflects the same brief but may differ in opening angle, phrasing, or emphasis.
Draft A
"Most coordination problems don't come from too few tools. They come from unclear ownership at the handoff."
Draft B
"Work rarely breaks because nobody is trying. It usually breaks in the handoff."
Draft C
"When a task changes hands, something important often doesn't move with it: clarity about who owns the next step."
2
Compare — judge which draft is closest to the brief
Tap to expand →
The team reads all three against the brief criteria — not "which one sounds best" but "which one is most on-brief." The comparison creates a shared judgment exercise that makes quality standards explicit.
Does the opening name the problem before the product?
Does the tone stay calm and systems-minded throughout?
Does it feel like it was written for the right audience?
Would the close create pressure or stay low-pressure?
3
Choose direction — select the strongest candidate
Tap to expand →
The team chooses the draft that is most structurally correct — not the most polished. A structurally correct draft with rough wording is easier to fix than a polished draft with the wrong message order. This is a human decision. The brief is the standard, not personal preference.
4
Revise — improve expression without changing strategy
Tap to expand →
Tighten wording. Sharpen sentences. The brief stays fixed. If a revision changes the audience, message order, or CTA logic — it went too far. Make it more precise, not more creative in a different direction.
5
Review and approve — final brief check before moving to Stage 5
Tap to expand →
One final check against the original brief before this moves to Stage 5.
Audience still correct
Message order still intact
Tone still on-brief — factual claims verified by a human
Watch for this

AI output can sound polished while still being strategically wrong. A draft that reads well but opens with the product instead of the problem, or ends with pressure language instead of a low-pressure close, is a failure — regardless of how smooth the writing is. Polish is not the review standard. Brief alignment is.
