What happens in each section

1
Introducing the Full Workplace Case and Its Objective
25 min · Context-setting
+
The session begins with one complete, realistic workplace case — a team with broken coordination, missing reporting, unmanaged repetition, and communication outputs that could not reach their audiences clearly.
The content establishes the business context, the problems that triggered the project, and the reasons the case required multiple tools and methods rather than one isolated fix.
Trainees interact with the problem set — identifying what type of operational failure each issue represents — before seeing the full five-stage solution map that the rest of the day walks through.
This section ends with a shared understanding of the case and a clear view of what the walkthrough will demonstrate at each stage.

2
Stage 1 Walkthrough: Workspace Setup and Structured Tracking
25 min · Days 1–2 revisited
+
The session moves into the first stage of the case — showing how structured tracking made the work visible before reporting, automation, or AI support could be introduced reliably.
The content revisits how Google Sheets, Notion, and Trello/ClickUp were used to define trackable fields, organize information consistently, and establish a shared operational view the team could actually use.
The content also covers why structure had to come before AI — without a clear, consistent record of what the work was, any AI-generated output at Stage 4 would have been built on guesswork.
This section ends with a clear view of the operational tracker as the foundation artifact — and why everything in Stages 2–5 depends on it being done correctly.
Stage 1 principle: Good tracking is not writing everything down. It is choosing the fields that make work controllable — and keeping them consistent enough to report from later.
3
Stage 2 Walkthrough: Reporting and Dashboard Production
30 min · Days 1–2 revisited
+
The session continues into how operational data from the tracker was transformed into a decision-facing leadership view — closing the gap between the team's working view and what leadership actually needed to see.
The content covers how KPIs were defined, how dashboard views were shaped around a specific decision need, and how interpretation was added to make the output useful rather than just data-complete.
The content also covers the gap between having a tracker and having a reporting layer — they serve different audiences and require different design thinking.
This section ends with a clear view of how operational data became a decision-ready dashboard — and why the tracker in Stage 1 had to be right before the dashboard in Stage 2 could be trusted.
Stage 2 principle: A useful dashboard does not show everything. It shows what helps someone act — and it removes the need for the team lead to answer questions that a well-designed view could answer automatically.
4
Stage 3 Walkthrough: Automation of Repeated Work
30 min · Days 2–3 revisited
+
The session shifts into the automation stage — showing how repeated coordination work that was still being done manually after Stages 1 and 2 was identified, mapped, and removed from the team's daily load.
The content covers the trigger → condition → action model for designing automation rules, how cross-tool movement was handled between Sheets, Notion, and task tools, and the reliability considerations that governed which tasks were suitable candidates.
The content also clarifies what automation did not do: it did not make the workflow smarter or replace human judgment — it made the workflow more reliable and gave time back.
This section ends with a clear understanding of where automation reduced manual repetition inside the case — and what the team was able to do with the time recovered.

Tasks that became automation candidates

A task is automatable when the trigger is clear, the condition is defined, and the action is predictable every time it runs:

  • Status change notifications sent when a field updates
  • Task assignment triggered when a new row is added
  • Weekly summary emails generated from tracker data
  • Cross-tool record creation when a form is submitted

Tasks that remained manual

Automation cannot replace judgment, contextual reading, or decisions that vary based on information the trigger cannot capture:

  • Deciding whether a status change represents a real problem or expected variance
  • Reviewing AI-generated output before it is used
  • Interpreting dashboard anomalies for leadership
  • Writing the rationale behind a communication decision

The right moment to automate

Automation only works reliably when three conditions are in place first:

  • The process is already working manually — automation of a broken process produces faster chaos
  • The trigger is consistent and detectable — the tool must know reliably when to act
  • The action is predictable — the same trigger should always produce the same response
5
Stage 4 Walkthrough: LLM-Supported Workplace Tasks
30 min · Day 4 revisited
+
The session moves into the AI stage — demonstrating why LLMs entered the workflow at Stage 4 rather than Stage 1, and what made them useful only once the brief was locked and the quality bar was already known.
The content covers the structured prompt workflow applied inside the case: task framing, context, format specification, constraints, and the review loop that ran before any AI output was accepted as a working draft.
The content also covers the generate-compare-check-revise cycle and the conditions under which an output was accepted, revised, or sent back for re-prompting.
This section ends with a practical view of how LLMs supported faster, more consistent first drafts inside the case — without removing the team's judgment at the review and revision stages.
Stage 4 principle: AI would have been weak at Stage 1 — there was nothing structured enough to work with. It became useful at Stage 4 because the brief was locked, the audience was known, and the team already understood what a good output looked like.
Try It Yourself

Before describing how you used AI in an interview or portfolio: explain what you had in place before the AI ran. The setup — the locked brief, the clear audience, the defined quality bar — is the skill. The AI is just faster execution of a process you already knew how to run.

6
Stage 5 Walkthrough: Communication and Deliverable Production
30 min · Day 5 revisited
+
The session continues into the final stage — showing how the case outcomes were turned into audience-facing deliverables that communicated the work clearly to people outside the team.
The content covers how message hierarchy, evidence, CTA, and brand consistency were applied across slide decks, one-pagers, and social assets — and how Canva AI supported production without overriding the communication strategy.
The content also covers the pre-review check that ran before the pack left the team — verifying that each deliverable served its communication purpose, not just its visual appearance.
This section ends with the complete five-stage workflow visible from end to end — and with a full picture of how each stage's output became the input for the next.
Stage 5 principle: Three deliverables only become a system when the same decisions run through all of them — same audience, same message logic, same tone, same CTA direction. Different format. Same strategy.
7
Comparing Manual Work and AI-Assisted Work Across the Case
20 min · Integration
+
The session focuses on an honest comparison between doing the case manually and doing it with structured AI support — covering where manual work remained necessary, where AI accelerated production, and how judgment was applied throughout both approaches.
The content covers the conditions under which AI adds genuine value (locked brief, clear audience, known quality bar) versus the conditions where manual control remains essential (ambiguous inputs, judgment-dependent decisions, safe-use checks).
This section ends with a clear mental model for choosing between manual and AI-assisted approaches — based on what the task actually requires, not on tool preference.

Closing with Reflection and Professional Packaging

The content covers a seven-part presentation framework for explaining the capstone case clearly: business context, problem identified, approach chosen, tools used, output produced, what changed, and what would be done differently.

The seven-part case presentation framework

1
Business Context
What the team was doing and what the overall work objective was — one or two sentences, no jargon.
2
Problem Identified
The specific operational gap that prompted the project — framed as a real problem, not a list of missing tools.
3
Approach Chosen
Why this approach — why tracking before reporting, why structure before AI — and what that sequence produced.
4
Tools Used
Named specifically, with one sentence on what each tool was used for in this case — not a general list of capabilities.
5
Output Produced
The real deliverables: tracker, dashboard, automation rules, AI-reviewed drafts, communication pack. Concrete and nameable.
6
What Changed
What became faster, more visible, or more reliable as a result — framed in operational terms, not in "I learned" terms.
7
What Would Be Done Differently
One honest observation — this shows professional judgment, not just execution. It is the detail that makes an explanation credible.
