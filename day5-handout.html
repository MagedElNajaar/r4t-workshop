What happens in each section

1
Defining the Communication Objective and Target Audience
25 min · Light concepts
+
The session begins with the business objective, the target audience, and the action or shift in understanding the deliverable is expected to create — before any visual or copy work begins.
The content covers how a vague goal like "promote the product" produces unfocused content, and how a specific communication objective shapes every production decision that follows.
Trainees compare two different audience definitions for the same product and explore how each one produces a completely different opening line, tone, message, and CTA — even though the product has not changed.
This section ends with a locked communication objective and a named target audience — the foundation everything else is built on.
Weak Objective
No reader named
No action defined
No outcome to measure
"Promote LaunchPad Pro" — could mean anything
Strong Objective
Names a specific reader
States the required action
Links to a measurable outcome
Constrains every production decision
2
Structuring the Message, Evidence, and CTA
30 min · Medium complexity
+
The session moves into the communication logic that must be in place before design or copy production begins — establishing the message hierarchy that will guide the entire deliverable set.
The content covers four components: the opening hook, the message flow and supporting points, evidence and KPI-linked claims, and the CTA that closes the content with direction.
The content also focuses on message sequence: good sentences in the wrong order still produce weak content. The order matters as much as the content itself.
This section ends with a complete message framework — ready to guide copy construction in the next section.
Opening
Hook the right reader — leads with something they already feel, not the product name. The reader must recognise the problem before the solution is introduced.
Message
Unfold the case — supporting points in sequence. Each line earns the right to the next one. No jump to the conclusion before the problem is fully established.
Evidence
Make it believable — a specific claim ("teams save 4.2 hours a week") is more persuasive than an impressive one. Evidence must connect to the reader's real experience.
CTA
Close with direction — the closing line continues the message, it does not pivot into selling mode. The reader should feel invited, not pressured.
3
Selecting the Deliverable Set and Building the First Draft Manually
55 min · Medium-heavy
+
The session focuses first on choosing the right anchor deliverable — not every output is an equally good starting point, and picking the wrong one compounds ambiguity across the whole set.
The content covers the relationship between the visual asset and the caption: they have different jobs and putting everything in one place weakens both. Asset carries the visual hook; caption carries the argument.
Trainees map the message hierarchy before writing any copy, then compare two first drafts — one that follows the structure and one that does not — to identify which is the stronger starting point and why.
This section ends with a first structured draft of the LinkedIn post — organised, message-led, and ready for AI-assisted refinement.
Try It Yourself

Before writing any copy: map the message hierarchy on paper first. What is the opening problem? What is the supporting point? What is the evidence? What is the closing action? Writing the sequence before writing the sentences produces a stronger first draft every time.

4
Using Canva AI and Structured Prompts for Production
40 min · Tool-heavy
+
The session shifts into AI-assisted production — but begins by identifying what parts of the draft AI is allowed to touch and what must stay fixed before a single prompt is written.
The content covers the anatomy of a production prompt: locked elements (audience, message, structure), improvable elements (phrasing, flow, CTA wording), and dangerous elements (audience definition, factual claims) that AI must not redefine.
Three AI-refined versions of the draft are compared — all look polished, but only one protects the brief. Trainees identify which one and trace every improvement back to a specific prompt decision.
This section ends with an AI-assisted LinkedIn working version that is stronger than the manual draft — and a clear record of what changed and why.
Anatomy of a production prompt for Canva AI
Audience: Operations managers at mid-size companies. Systems-minded. Not salesy.
Message: Coordination overhead is the real problem. LaunchPad Pro removes it.
Tone: Calm, credible, professional. Never excited. Never corporate-jargon.
Locked: 4-part structure, specific evidence stat, audience language.
Improve: Opening sharpness, sentence flow, CTA specificity.
Do NOT: Change the audience definition or rewrite the evidence claim.
5
Applying Brand Constraints and Visual Consistency
30 min · Medium complexity
+
The session focuses on adapting the LinkedIn working version for Instagram — making it clear that this is a format adaptation, not a campaign rewrite. The message strategy stays fixed; the format decisions change.
The content covers eight specific adaptation decisions — what changes between platforms (format, length, visual register, caption style) and what must stay fixed (audience, message hierarchy, evidence, CTA direction).
The content also covers brand kits, logo use, typography rules, color discipline, and the role of templates in keeping multiple assets visually aligned without starting from scratch each time.
This section ends with a locked Instagram working version — adapted correctly from the LinkedIn source, visually consistent, and ready to accompany the rationale sheet.

What stays fixed across both platforms

The communication strategy does not change when the format does. These elements are locked in both LinkedIn and Instagram versions:

  • Target audience definition and pain point
  • Four-part message hierarchy (hook → case → evidence → CTA)
  • Specific evidence claim (the number, not just a vague claim)
  • CTA direction and brand voice

What changes for Instagram

Format adaptation means changing the delivery — not the message. These decisions are different on Instagram:

  • Visual asset format (square or portrait, not landscape)
  • Caption length (shorter, hook-first)
  • Sentence rhythm (punchier, less discursive)
  • Opening line construction (visual hook leads, not a problem statement)

Brand controls that keep the set consistent

Multiple deliverables only feel like a campaign if they are visually aligned. Brand controls that matter:

  • Canva brand kit — approved fonts, colors, logo files in one place
  • Templates — set the layout once, apply across formats
  • Typography rules — one display font, one body font, consistent sizing
  • Color discipline — brand palette only, no ad hoc color choices
6
Reviewing Deliverables for Usefulness, Not Decoration
25 min · Medium complexity
+
The session emphasises that an attractive deliverable is not the same as an effective one — and that portfolio-ready means communication-ready, not just visually polished.
The content covers building a campaign rationale sheet — the document that explains every decision in decision language: why this audience, why this structure, why this message, why this CTA.
Trainees classify what belongs in a rationale sheet, compare preference language versus decision language for each section, and lock a final version that can sit alongside the assets in a portfolio or brand review submission.
This section ends with a complete, portfolio-ready rationale sheet — the third deliverable in the campaign set, and the one that demonstrates strategic control over the other two.
Review Criterion What It Tests Standard
Objective alignment Does the deliverable serve the stated communication objective? Must pass
Message hierarchy Is the four-part structure visible and in the right order? Must pass
Evidence Is the supporting claim specific, credible, and audience-relevant? Must pass
CTA Does it continue the message naturally — without pivoting to ad language? Required
Visual consistency Do both assets feel like they belong to the same campaign? Check
Rationale language test: If a sentence in your rationale says "we chose this because it looks good" — rewrite it. Decision language says "we chose this because the target reader responds to credibility signals, not visual excitement."
7
Managing Versions, Variants, and Audience Adaptation
25 min · Medium complexity
+
The session moves into the production discipline required when a campaign needs more than one version of an asset — and explains why this is harder than it looks once a second audience or channel is introduced.
The content covers how to adjust the entry point for different audience variants while keeping the campaign core stable: same message, same evidence, same CTA direction — different opening, different tone register, different visual lead.
The content also covers versioning discipline and review cycles — tracking what changed between drafts, what was approved, and what was rejected — so the team is never working from an outdated or unapproved asset.
This section ends with a clearer approach to controlled iteration: producing multiple audience variants without losing the original communication objective.

The Adaptation Rule — click to explore

What the campaign core must protect
Tap to reveal
The message, the evidence claim, the CTA direction, and the brand voice. These do not change between variants. If they do, you are not adapting — you are starting a different campaign.
What the entry point is allowed to shift
Tap to reveal
The opening hook, the pain point framing, the specific language register, and the visual lead. These adapt to the reader — but the argument underneath them stays unchanged.
When a variant becomes a different campaign
Tap to reveal
When the audience definition changes fundamentally. When the message hierarchy is reordered. When the evidence claim is replaced. When the CTA asks for a different action. At that point, it is a new brief — not a variant.
8
Using AI to Refine Communication Quality and Output Range
20 min · Tool-heavy
+
The session closes by applying structured AI prompting to a second, harder task — using what was learned in Sections 4 and 5 to produce audience-specific variants more efficiently, with sharper prompt control.
The content covers practical prompting for message tightening, alternative CTA generation, audience-specific variant production, and final communication polishing across the deliverable set.
Three AI-drafted LinkedIn variants are compared — all plausible, only one genuinely follows the brief. Trainees apply the review criteria from Section 6 to identify which draft passes and which ones fail at the message level despite looking polished.
The session closes with a complete, reviewable AI-assisted marketing workflow — brief, message framework, two assets, rationale sheet, and a production log — ready for portfolio submission or brand review.
Try It Yourself

Before asking AI to generate a variant: write the locked elements first. List what AI cannot change. Then specify what it should improve. A constrained prompt produces a usable result. An open prompt produces a different campaign.

The final question of Day 5: "Did we produce something portfolio-worthy?" — not "Did we use AI?" A portfolio-worthy deliverable demonstrates that every decision was made deliberately: audience, message, evidence, CTA, format, visual register, and rationale.
