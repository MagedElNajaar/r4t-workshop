Section 2

Detailed Outlines

Each stage below shows the flow of the session: how it opens, what it covers, and what concrete outcome it reaches. Click any card to expand it.

1
Converting the Day 1 backlog into Jira issue structure
40 min
▼
  • The session opens with the planning-ready backlog produced during Day 1 — all scope decisions, story states, and readiness flags are already in place before any Jira work begins.
  • The content moves that backlog into Jira by establishing the correct hierarchy: epicsEpics group work by capability area — e.g. "Routing Logic" or "Agent Visibility" group capabilities, storiesStories describe a user-facing outcome: what someone can do as a result of this work describe user outcomes, and tasksTasks are discrete implementation steps that sit beneath a story carry the implementation detail.
  • Partial and blocked items are explicitly placed inside the hierarchy — marked, not removed — so the team can see what exists without treating unready items as executable.
  • The difference between a structured hierarchy and a flat ticket dump is examined directly: what each approach makes visible, and at what cost.
  • This stage ends with the full backlog represented in Jira as a structured, navigable hierarchy — not a flat list.
🎯

Outcome: A Jira project containing epics, stories, and tasks that reflect the scope, structure, and readiness states from Day 1 — without flattening or losing the decisions already made.

2
Organizing and ranking the backlog in Jira
35 min
▼
  • The session moves from structure into order. The hierarchy is in place — the question becomes what should rise to the top and why.
  • The content introduces three ordering lenses that must be applied together: importance (how much does this matter?), executability (can the team actually start this now?), and dependency order (does something else need to exist before this can move?).
  • The session challenges the assumption that "most important" equals "top of the backlog" — a blocked high-priority item at the top adds pressure without enabling any progress.
  • Learners rank the real items from the Support Escalation backlog before the correct order is revealed, making the judgment explicit and discussable.
  • This stage ends with a backlog that is ordered, reasoned, and ready to be pulled from — the top reflects what the team can actually execute first.

The three ordering lenses

The most commonly applied lens — but not sufficient on its own. An item can be critical to the product and still unmovable because it is blocked, incomplete, or waiting for a dependency. Importance determines long-term priority; the other two lenses determine what can move now.
Is the scope defined? Are acceptance criteria written? Is there a named owner? Is anything blocking it? Executability is the gate. An item that fails this check should not sit at the top of the backlog, regardless of how important it is in the abstract.
Two items with equal importance are not interchangeable if one depends on the other. The item that unlocks later work rises — not because it is more valuable, but because the dependency order determines what can happen in what sequence.
🎯

Outcome: A backlog that is ordered by what can actually move — not just what the team cares most about in the abstract.

3
Defining workflow statuses and board logic
35 min
▼
  • The session establishes what each workflow status actually means — not as labels, but as shared agreements about readiness, progress, and completion.
  • Six statuses are defined with consistent, team-agreed meanings: Intake, Ready, In Progress, Review, Blocked, and Done.
  • The content clarifies the movement rules between statuses — what allows an item to advance, and what should prevent it from moving forward prematurely.
  • Blocked is examined closely: it requires a named blocker. Blocked without a cause is not a valid state.
  • Done is examined with equal rigor: it means verified and closed — not self-assessed as complete.
  • This stage ends with a shared status vocabulary and clear movement logic that the whole team can operate from consistently.

Workflow status reference

Intake
Item exists but has not yet been assessed for readiness. It is visible — not executable.
Ready
Scope defined, ACs written, owner named, no active blockers. Can enter the sprint.
In Progress
Work has started. A named owner is actively working on it inside the sprint.
Review
Work is complete but awaiting verification — a code review, QA check, or approval step.
Blocked
Cannot proceed. A named blocker must be specified — unresolved external dependency, decision, or missing input.
Done
Verified and closed. Not self-assessed — confirmed against ACs by someone other than the author.
🎯

Outcome: A workflow with six named statuses, shared definitions, and agreed movement rules — so the board reflects delivery reality rather than guesswork.

4
Building the board view for delivery visibility
35 min
▼
  • The session turns the defined workflow into a physical board view that the team can operate from every day.
  • The content examines what a useful board shows — ownership, priority, blockers, and work status — and what a noisy board shows instead: excessive metadata that competes with the signal the team actually needs.
  • Two board views are compared side by side: one overloaded with labels, tags, and fields the team never reads during a standup; one focused on the four questions that matter in daily delivery.
  • The four daily questions the board must answer quickly are addressed: What is moving? What is blocked? Who owns it? What needs attention now?
  • This stage ends with a board configured to answer those questions clearly — without requiring a verbal explanation from the PM to make sense of.
Try it yourself

Look at the board you use (or have seen in your team). Can you answer all four questions in under 10 seconds by looking at it? If not — what is getting in the way?

1. What is moving? — Items in In Progress, with a named owner.
2. What is blocked? — Items in Blocked, with a named cause.
3. Who owns it? — Visible on every active ticket without opening it.
4. What needs attention now? — Blocked items, items in Review too long, items approaching a deadline.

🎯

Outcome: A board view that supports daily standup, surfaces blockers at a glance, and makes ownership visible without requiring anyone to decode it.

5
Preparing sprint-ready work and creating the first sprint
45 min
▼
  • The session moves from backlog order into near-term execution planning — the step where what should happen becomes what the team actually commits to.
  • The content covers sprint-ready selection: which items meet the criteria to enter the sprint (scoped, unblocked, owned, sized) versus which items must wait.
  • Dependency awareness is applied at the sprint level: items that unblock later work are prioritized for the first sprint even if individually they feel smaller than other candidates.
  • The session covers the mechanics of creating the first sprint in Jira — moving items from the ordered backlog top into a bounded sprint with a goal, a scope, and a start date.
  • The session distinguishes a sprint from a wish list: a sprint is a real commitment, not everything the team hopes to complete soon.
  • This stage ends with a first sprint created in Jira — bounded, executable, and drawn from the structured backlog.

6
Managing blockers, handoffs, and delivery controls
25 min
▼
  • The session focuses on how the workflow handles the friction that always appears during live delivery: work that gets blocked, work that needs to move between owners, and work that must pass a review gate before it can close.
  • The content keeps the emphasis on delivery control — not project management theory. The goal is a workflow that makes problems visible before they cause delays.
  • Blocked work is examined as a signal, not a status: when an item moves to Blocked, the cause should be visible without opening the ticket.
  • Handoff moments — where one person completes and another must pick up — are mapped into the workflow so they do not become invisible gaps.
  • This stage ends with a workflow configured to surface friction early and support smoother execution across the team — not just track tickets.
Delivery control principle

A blocked item with no named cause is not a delivery signal — it is just a badge. The workflow's job is to make the cause visible, not confirm that someone remembered to change a status.

🎯

Outcome: A workflow that makes blockers, review states, and handoff points visible during execution — so the team can respond before delays compound.

7
Adding basic dashboards and lightweight automation
25 min
▼
  • The session introduces the minimum reporting and automation layer needed to keep the workspace usable without becoming a full-time maintenance job.
  • The content covers simple dashboard elements that give the team and stakeholders an accurate read on sprint progress, blocked count, and items in review — without requiring manual status updates.
  • Basic automation rules are introduced to reduce the manual tracking that causes drift: auto-transitions, assignment triggers, and notification rules that keep the board reflecting reality.
  • The session frames dashboards as a visibility tool — not a reporting theater. A dashboard that requires data entry to stay accurate is not serving the team.
  • This stage ends with a Jira workspace that is genuinely usable for live execution, team coordination, and stakeholder follow-up — with minimal manual overhead.
Discussion prompt

What is the minimum information a dashboard needs to show for a stakeholder to understand the sprint's health at a glance — without being walked through it?

Sprint goal and whether the team is on track to meet it. Items remaining vs. items closed. Blocked count and named blockers. Any item in Review for more than two days. That is enough for a meaningful check-in — anything else is noise.

🎯

Outcome: A lightweight dashboard and at least two automation rules that keep the workspace accurate without requiring someone to manually maintain it.
