What Day 2 is — and is not

Before building anything, understand the correct mindset for this day.

Not this
  • Learning tools before defining the question
  • Adding charts because they look good
  • Treating all numbers as equally important
  • Building dashboards as proof of work
  • Using AI to generate analysis without data
  • Presenting chart behavior instead of business impact
→

  • Start with a specific business question
  • Define KPIs before opening any tool
  • Clean and structure data for your KPI model

StyleHub — the case you will work through

Every example today is anchored in this scenario. Study the situation — do not jump to conclusions.

The company

StyleHub is an online fashion e-commerce business. It tracks website traffic, conversion rates, revenue, and order data across multiple marketing channels.

Online fashion Multi-channel E-commerce

Management has noticed a performance issue. Your job is to investigate using the data — not to assume you already know the answer.

Traffic is up. Orders are down. Conversion rate has dropped significantly.

The CEO's question

"Our website traffic is increasing but sales are not following. What is happening and what should we do about it?"

1 Section 1

Framing the Reporting Question

Before touching any tool, define what you are trying to answer, who is reading it, and what decision it must support.

Core rule: A dashboard is not "show me all the data." It is "help me answer this exact question." If you cannot state the question in one sentence, you are not ready to build.

Observation vs. Question — know the difference

Observation (not enough)
  • "Sales are dropping."
  • "Traffic is up but revenue is down."
  • Cannot be built from — too vague to direct a dashboard
Analytical Question (actionable)
  • "Why is conversion rate declining despite rising traffic?"
  • Specific · Measurable · Points to the data needed
  • Every design decision that follows can be tested against it

Audience determines everything — click each to compare

CEO View Executive
Wants: instant summary + revenue impact. Needs: 3 KPIs visible in 5 seconds. Question: "Is the business improving or not?" Action: "What do we prioritize this week?" — Build for understanding, not investigation.
Marketing Manager Operational
Wants: channel breakdowns + campaign ROI. Needs: filters by channel, date, audience segment. Question: "Which campaign is underperforming?" Action: "Which campaign do I pause or fix?" — Build for investigation and action.
Day 2 focus: The dashboard you build today is CEO-facing. Interpretation work in Section 6 is the analyst's job — reading that dashboard to form a recommendation.

Data → Metrics → KPIs — three different layers

Raw Data
order_id · price · channel
Facts from the system. No meaning on their own.
→
Metrics
Total Revenue · Order Count
Calculations applied to data. Useful but lack context.
→
KPIs
Conversion Rate by Channel
Metric + Context + Purpose. Only these belong at the top of a dashboard.

The dashboard purpose statement

Write one sentence before building anything. Every chart, metric, and filter must serve this statement.

StyleHub Purpose Statement

"To identify where and why conversion rate is decreasing across channels and customer segments, in order to support decisions that improve sales performance."

Formula: To [identify/understand] [KPI] across [dimensions], in order to support [specific decision].
2 Section 2

KPI Model & Data Dictionary

Every KPI needs four components. Without all four, the number will be interpreted differently by different people.

The KPI test: Ask "What would someone DO differently because of this number?" If you cannot answer — it is a metric, not a KPI.

Every KPI has four required components

Definition
Plain-language explanation of what it measures and what it excludes. Revenue = total completed order value, excluding refunds.
Formula
The exact calculation. Conversion Rate = COUNT(completed orders) ÷ COUNT(unique sessions)
Dimensions
The "by" fields — how it can be broken down. By Channel, by Customer Type, by Time Period.
Filters
What can be included or excluded. Date range, channel selection, customer segment.

StyleHub KPI set — click each to see the definition

Conversion Rate Primary KPI
Definition: % of sessions that result in a completed purchase.
Formula: COUNT(orders) ÷ COUNT(sessions)
Dimension: By Channel, by Customer Type
Why it matters: Tells you where traffic is failing to convert — not just that it is.
Revenue Financial KPI
Definition: Total value of completed orders in the period, excluding refunded transactions.
Formula: SUM(completed order values)
Dimension: By month, by channel, by product category
Total Orders Volume KPI
Definition: Count of completed purchase transactions in the period.
Formula: COUNT(Order_ID where status = Completed)
Dimension: By channel, by customer type, by date
Avg Order Value Efficiency KPI
Definition: Average revenue generated per completed order.
Formula: Total Revenue ÷ Total Orders
Dimension: By channel, by product category

Dimensions — the "where" and "who" of your data

Dimensions are the fields that break down a metric. Without dimensions, a KPI only tells you what happened. With dimensions, it tells you where and who.

Channel

Facebook · Google · Organic · Email. Reveals where traffic originates and how each source converts.

Customer Type

New vs. Returning. Reveals whether the issue affects acquisition, retention, or both.

Time Period

Day · Week · Month. Reveals when the change started and whether it is a trend or a single event.

Mini data dictionary — why it prevents disagreements

The problem: If one analyst counts all page views as "visits" and another counts only unique sessions, they produce different conversion rates from the same data — and both are technically right. A data dictionary stops this before it happens.
Minimum entry per KPI field
Field name: What the column is called in the cleaned dataset
Definition: What it measures and what it excludes
Formula: How it is calculated (exact logic)
Type: Dimension or Measure
Valid values: For dimensions — the agreed controlled list
Rule: If two analysts can calculate the same KPI with different answers, the data dictionary is missing.
3 Section 3

Preparing Data for Reporting

Clean data is necessary but not sufficient. The structure must also support your KPI model — before you open any BI tool.

Core principle: Garbage in → Garbage out. The most polished Power BI dashboard built on uncleaned data produces wrong conclusions with complete confidence.

What uncleaned data does to your dashboard

Before cleaning
After cleaning
  • 3 separate entries for the same channel: fb / Facebook / FB
  • "Cairo" / "cairo" / "CAIRO" counted as 3 different cities
  • Shoes and Footwear treated as separate categories — Footwear looks tiny
  • Duplicate sessions inflate apparent conversion rate
  • Missing customer_type values — segment analysis unavailable
  • Result: CEO draws conclusions from a distorted picture
  • One unified "Facebook" channel across all records
  • Standardized city values — one entry per location
  • Shoes + Footwear merged — category now shows true size
  • Duplicates removed — conversion rate is accurate
  • Missing values handled — full segment breakdown available
  • Result: CEO makes a decision grounded in correct data

Data cleaning checklist — 5 required steps

Step 1 · Standardise naming
Same value must have exactly the same label everywhere. Facebook = Facebook — not fb, FB, or facebook. Category names must match across all records.
Step 2 · Handle missing values
Every missing value is a decision. Fill with logic where possible (e.g. infer customer type from order history). Remove and document when inference is not safe. Never leave gaps undocumented.
Step 3 · Merge split categories
When the same real-world category appears under multiple names, merge them into one controlled value. Hidden performance often surfaces when this is done.
Step 4 · Fix data types
Revenue stored as text cannot be summed. Dates stored as free text cannot be filtered by period. Every field must be in the correct format for the formula that will use it.
Step 5 · Remove unnecessary columns
Every field in the cleaned dataset must serve a KPI or a dimension. Unused columns add noise and risk confusion. If it does not contribute to your model — remove it.

From cleaned data to report-ready structure

Cleaning is not enough. Your data must also be structured so that each KPI formula can be calculated at the correct level of detail. This is the bridge between data prep and dashboard build.
Dimensions — the "by" fields
  • Fields used to slice your data
  • Channel · Customer Type · Product · Date
  • Answer: "Where?" and "Who?"
  • Must be standardised and consistent
Measures — the "what" fields
  • Fields you calculate to produce a KPI
  • Revenue · Order Count · Conversion Rate
  • Answer: "How much?" and "How many?"
  • Must be calculable at the dimension level
StyleHub report-readiness check
✓  Channel column present and standardised
✓  Customer_Type column present with consistent values
✓  Order_Date in date format — filterable by week/month
✓  Visits and Orders at the same time and channel granularity
✓  Conversion Rate can be calculated by channel without ambiguity
Rule: If your visits and orders are at different time granularities, stop. Fix the structure before building anything.

Chart type by question — not by preference

KPI Card
Single most-critical number. Use for the top row. Requires immediate visibility.
Line Chart
Change over time. Use when the trend pattern matters more than individual values.
Bar Chart
Comparison across categories. Use to rank channels, segments, or products by a measure.
Table
Detailed record lookup. Use only when exact values must be read — not for pattern recognition.
Dashboard pitfalls to avoid
Too many visuals — no hierarchy, no clear story
Charts chosen for appearance, not purpose
No filters or too many filters (CEO limit: 3 max)
KPIs buried — not immediately visible
Pie charts for magnitude comparison — use bars
Design should support understanding — not serve as decoration.

Validate before sharing

The dashboard passes when all of these are true
✓  Someone can see whether conversion is improving or declining in under 10 seconds
✓  Someone can see when the decline started
✓  Someone can identify which channel or segment is driving the change
✓  Every visual serves the purpose statement — nothing extra
✓  The CEO can answer the question without analyst support
If something does not answer the purpose statement — remove it. Simplicity is discipline, not laziness.
5 Section 5

Building in Tools — Power BI + Looker Studio

Tools execute the logic you have already defined. Power BI is the main build. Looker Studio is a translation — not a second full build.

The key principle: If you needed to relearn everything for each new tool, you did not really understand dashboards. The logic — question, KPIs, layout — is tool-independent.

Power BI — build reference

In Power BI, your KPIs become Measures created in the Modeling tab. Your layout plan from Section 4 becomes the report canvas. Your dimensions become the fields you use for slicers and chart axes.

Core DAX Measures — StyleHub
Total Revenue = SUM(Sales[Revenue])
Total Orders = COUNT(Sales[Order_ID])
Avg Order Value = DIVIDE([Total Revenue], [Total Orders])
Conversion Rate = DIVIDE([Total Orders], [Total Visits])
What changes between tools
  • Where to click to create a measure or calculated field
  • How filters (slicers vs. filter controls) are added
  • How to connect and refresh your data source
  • The default color palette and publishing method
What never changes
  • The business question and purpose statement
  • All KPI definitions and formulas
  • The layout logic (KPIs → Trend → Breakdowns → Filters)
  • The dimensions and filter fields

6 Section 6

Interpretation, Narrative & Action

This is where you shift from dashboard builder to analyst. Your job is to explain what the data means and what it demands — from the CEO-facing dashboard you built.

The critical gap: Anyone can build charts. The analyst who can explain what they mean — and recommend action specifically — is the one who earns trust.

Describe business impact — not chart behavior

Describing the chart
  • "The line in the conversion rate chart goes down."
  • Forces the reader to interpret it themselves
  • Tells them nothing about consequence or urgency
Describing the business
  • "Conversion rate has fallen 38%, from 3.4% to 2.1% — a drop that directly reduces the revenue generated per visitor."
  • States scale, timeframe, and business consequence
  • CEO immediately understands the size of the problem

Four pattern types to look for in every breakdown

Trends

Is a metric moving consistently up or down over time? A 5-week decline is different from a single bad week — it points to a structural issue, not a one-time event.

Anomalies

A sudden spike or drop that breaks the pattern. Anomalies often correlate with a specific event: a campaign launch, a technical issue, a promotional period.

Comparisons

How does one channel compare to another? How does this month compare to last? Comparisons reveal relative performance that single numbers hide.

Contributions

Which segment or channel is driving the overall change? A decline concentrated in one channel tells a very different story than an even decline across all channels.

Pattern vs. Proof — a critical professional distinction. Dashboards reveal concentration points and suggest likely causes — they do not prove causation. A strong correlation is a hypothesis to test, not a confirmed conclusion. Frame recommendations as: "The data strongly suggests X — we recommend action Y to test and validate this direction."

The four-step narrative framework

01 · What happened
State the change with scale
"Conversion rate has declined 38% over two months, from 3.4% to 2.1%."
02 · Where
Locate the concentration
"The drop is concentrated in one specific traffic source and among new customers."
03 · Why likely
Offer a grounded hypothesis
"The pattern suggests a possible acquisition or first-visit experience issue — new users are arriving but not completing purchases."
04 · What to do
One specific action to test
"Audit audience targeting and A/B test landing page variations. Measure conversion improvement within 4 weeks."

Specific recommendations earn trust. Vague ones do not.

Vague — avoids commitment
  • "We should improve our marketing."
  • "Review the targeting strategy."
  • Gives impression of analysis without substance
Specific — grounded and testable
  • Names the channel and the issue
  • States the test to run
  • Includes a time horizon to measure success
Rule: If your analysis does not end with a specific, testable action — it is not finished.
7 Section 7

AI Support & Day 2 Output

AI accelerates the communication layer of analysis. It does not replace your thinking, your data, or your judgment.

Where AI fits: Data → KPIs → Dashboard → Interpretation ↔ AI → Communication ↔ AI. AI adds value at the last two stages only — after your analytical foundation is in place.

What AI can genuinely help with — in the Day 2 workflow

Refining KPI names

AI can sharpen a rough KPI label so it clearly reflects the business question it serves. The definition must be correct first — AI improves the wording, not the logic.

Critiquing dashboard structure

Describe your dashboard layout and purpose statement to AI and ask it to identify gaps. It can spot missing KPIs or filter options that do not serve the CEO's question.

Drafting an executive summary

After you have identified key patterns, AI can draft a professional summary based on your dashboard findings. You validate it against the data before using it.

Sharpening recommendation phrasing

AI can help frame a vague conclusion as a specific, data-grounded recommendation with a testable action — once you have supplied the analytical finding it should be based on.

Good prompt vs. weak prompt — the formula matters

Weak prompt
"Write an executive summary about our marketing performance."
No data. No context. No format guidance. AI will produce something generic and ungrounded that cannot be used.
Strong prompt
"You are a business analyst for an online fashion e-commerce company. Traffic increased 20%, conversion rate dropped from 3.4% to 2.1%, concentrated in one specific channel. Write a 3-sentence executive summary with one specific recommendation. Plain business language."
Context + Data + Task + Format = usable output every time.
What AI cannot do: Know your organization's internal context or priorities. Know what your CEO cares about this week. Validate outputs against real data. Own or be accountable for a recommendation. The analyst owns all of these. AI is a tool that makes a skilled analyst faster — it does not make an unskilled person into an analyst.

What You Should Be Able to Produce After Day 2

These are the five concrete outputs a working analyst produces from this day's process. Not reflections — real deliverables.

  • 01
    A one-sentence dashboard purpose statement
    States what the dashboard is for, who it serves, and what decision it supports. Filters every design choice.
  • 02
    A KPI list with definitions
    3–5 KPIs. Each with: name, plain-language definition, exact formula, dimension, and target or benchmark.
  • 03
    A cleaned data checklist or mini data dictionary
    Documents every cleaning decision with before/after. Confirms data is report-ready: right granularity, right structure, right field names.
  • 04
    A dashboard wireframe or layout plan
    Shows what goes where and why. Each visual tied to a specific part of the purpose statement. Not a design — a justified plan.
  • 05
    A short executive summary with one recommendation
    3–5 sentences. Follows: What happened → Where → Why likely → What to do. Includes a time horizon to measure impact.
How to know your output is strong
✓  The purpose statement names a specific decision — not just a topic
✓  Every KPI has a formula that produces the same result regardless of who runs it
✓  The data checklist explains structural readiness — not just that it was "cleaned"
✓  The dashboard layout shows a clear hierarchy: status, then trend, then breakdown
✓  The executive summary separates what the data shows from what you believe it means

  • Observations are not questions

KPI Rule
