! Important

This is not a PRD editing lesson. It is a translation lesson.

The goal today is not to make a weak document look better. It is to follow the full path from business need to engineering action — in the correct order, without skipping the layer in the middle.

✕ Not this
  • Editing a weak PRD to make it prettier
  • Writing tickets from a business brief
  • Estimating before scope is bounded
  • Assuming engineering can fix missing product decisions
  • Treating "we have a document" as "we are ready to build"
→
✓ Yes this
  • Reading the BRD as the business layer — nothing more
  • Writing the PRD from the business requirement
  • Bounding scope before any engineering work is structured
  • Naming unknowns, risks, and open questions explicitly
  • Building the engineering plan only after the PRD is clear
The correct sequence: Read the BRD correctly → Write the PRD from it → Bound scope and surface risks → Build engineering structure → Write acceptance criteria → Shape delivery → Build the action plan
📋 Use Case

The same scenario. All day.

Every section uses this use case. Keep it in mind at each layer.

The business problem

A SaaS company receives customer-reported issues through Slack, email, spreadsheets, and direct messages. Important issues are delayed, duplicated, or escalated to engineering without enough information to act on.

Support Customer Success Engineering Leadership

The business goal

Build a lightweight internal workflow for issue intake, urgency classification, routing, and status tracking — without introducing unnecessary process overhead.

V1 outcome targets
Faster triage · Fewer incomplete escalations · Clearer status visibility
0 Section 0

The Missing Translation Step

A document labelled as a PRD arrived. The team's first job is to classify it — which layer is it actually operating at?

Core idea: Business intent is not product specification. Engineering cannot plan responsibly from the first. The missing layer between them — the PRD — must be written, not assumed.

The three layers — click each to see what it does

BRD Business layer
Captures the business problem, the desired outcome, stakeholders, and constraints. Does not define system behavior, workflow rules, or user-facing logic. Answers: "Why do we need this?"
PRD Product layer
Translates business intent into how the system must behave. Defines user needs, workflow rules, states, permissions, required fields, and measurable success conditions. Answers: "How must the system work?"
Engineering Plan Execution layer
Converts the clarified PRD into epics, stories, tasks, acceptance criteria, and a delivery sequence. Only safe to begin after the PRD layer is specific enough to plan from. Answers: "What do we build, in what order, and how do we know when it's done?"
BRD · Business Layer

What the business needs and why

  • Business problem and pain
  • Desired outcome
  • Who is affected
  • Operating constraints
  • Success at business level
PRD · Product Layer

How the system must behave

  • User needs per user type
  • Workflow behavior and rules
  • States, permissions, fields
  • Scope and non-goals
  • Measurable success conditions
Engineering Plan · Execution

What gets built and how

  • Epics from scope decisions
  • Stories from user needs
  • Tasks from implementation
  • AC and readiness checks
  • Delivery sequence and action plan
"BRD: what the business needs and why. PRD: how the system must behave. Engineering planning begins only after the second document exists."
The v0.6 document that arrived — "PRD v0.6 — Support Escalation Workflow" — is still operating at the business layer. It has labels, steps, and capability names, but no workflow rules, no state definitions, no required fields, and no measurable success conditions. The PRD layer was never written.
1 Section 1 — The BRD Layer

Reading the Business Requirement Correctly

Before the PRD can be written, engineering must read the business brief as what it is — the BRD layer. Extract only what it explicitly gives. Name what is implied. Name what is missing.

Core idea: A BRD is the starting material for a PRD — not a planning document itself. Reading it correctly means stopping at what it actually says, not filling in the gaps silently.

This use case: the business brief gives us

Business problem

No standardized intake process. Issues arrive through inconsistent channels. Urgent issues are hard to identify. Engineering receives incomplete reports. Support has no status visibility after escalation.

Business goal

Structured intake, urgency classification, correct routing, and status tracking — lightweight, usable by non-engineering staff, no full platform migration.

Stakeholders

Support (submitters), Customer Success (visibility + follow-up), Engineering (triage + resolution), Managers (workload oversight), Leadership (aggregate visibility). How they hand off to each other is not yet defined.

Constraints

V1 must be lightweight and practical. No full platform migration. Must work for non-engineering staff. Should not create heavy manual overhead. Must be adoptable quickly.

Explicit / Implied / Missing — reading analysis

Business Brief — Layer Classification
✓ Explicitly stated
Issue intake is inconsistent across channels
Urgent issues are hard to identify and prioritize
Engineering receives incomplete reports
Support has no status visibility after escalation
Leadership needs visibility into escalated issue volume
V1 must be lightweight — no full platform migration
Must be usable by non-engineering staff
~ Implied but not confirmed
Some form of role-based workflow
Some urgency or priority classification model
An internal review or assignment process within engineering
Status visible across some teams after escalation

✕ Missing entirely
What fields are required to submit an escalation
Who sets severity — and by what rules
Role permissions — who can change, assign, or close
Triage logic — what happens during review

Do not assume: Who sets priority · That support owns severity · That status names mean what you think · A specific tool · Automation in V1 · Customer-facing visibility · Duplicate handling in V1 · That success criteria are measurable as stated.
"If the business brief does not say it, engineering does not get to quietly invent it."
2 Section 2 — Writing the PRD

Translating Business Language into Product Structure

The BRD layer is read. Now write the PRD from it — translating business intent into product-facing structure that engineering can begin to plan from.

Core idea: Business language describes pain. Product language describes what the workflow must make possible. Writing one when you mean the other is how teams end up with documents that look like PRDs but are still operating at the business layer.

The translation — business pain → product behavior

Language Transformation — Business Brief → PRD
Business language — describes pain
Critical issues are hard to identify and prioritize
Engineering receives incomplete reports before triage
Support has no status visibility after escalation
Teams interpret severity differently
Leadership needs better visibility
Product language — describes what the workflow must enable
The workflow must support urgency classification using a shared rule model — rules to be defined
Issue submission must capture the minimum context required for triage — required fields to be defined
Non-engineering stakeholders must be able to view current status without operational access
The workflow must expose a consistent issue state model readable across all teams
The system must provide visibility appropriate to each stakeholder type

User needs — stakeholder list → product-relevant need

Support Agent

Primary

Needs a structured submission format that enforces the minimum information engineering needs — without requiring engineering knowledge to complete.

Engineering Reviewer

Primary

Needs enough intake context to triage without sending repeated clarification requests before work can begin.

Customer Success

Secondary

Needs post-escalation status visibility — current state, last change, current owner — without engineering tooling access.

Eng Manager / PM

Secondary

Needs queue and assignment state visibility — what is escalated, assigned, and potentially stalled — at a queue level, not just per issue.

Leadership / Ops

Visibility only

Needs aggregate summary visibility — volume of open escalations, urgent issues being addressed — not individual workflow detail or operational access.

"A first PRD draft from the BRD is not the same as a complete, planning-ready product requirement. The translation started. It is not finished."
3 Section 3 — Completing the PRD

Defining Scope and Non-Goals

The PRD has structure. Engineering still cannot estimate without knowing what is explicitly inside this requirement and what is explicitly outside it.

Core idea: Scope is not the feature list. It is the explicit boundary between what this version commits to building and what the team is deliberately refusing to absorb. Both sides must be written.

V1 In-Scope — explicit commitments

V1 In-Scope — Support Escalation Workflow
Structured Intake
Consistent submission format replacing ad-hoc channels. Required fields TBD.
Priority Classification
Shared model (Low / Medium / High / Critical). Rules and ownership TBD.
Engineering Review
Defined decision point: accept, return for more context, or defer. Tracked state transition.
Assignment
One visible engineering owner per accepted issue, with logged timestamp.
Status Tracking
Current state visible to submitters and CS after escalation. State definitions TBD.
Queue Visibility
List view for engineering managers — incoming, in review, assigned, stalled. Not a dashboard.
Resolution State
Defined closed state with logged timestamp. Resolution criteria TBD.

V1 Non-Goals — deliberate exclusions

✕ Customer-facing visibility — V1 is internal only. External status or notifications belong in a later version.
✕ Automated priority classification — Rules not yet defined. Automating undefined rules creates automated inconsistency.
✕ External platform integrations — No Zendesk, Linear, Jira, or other platform integration in V1. Standalone internal process.
✕ SLA tracking or escalation timers — SLA targets not defined. Timer logic adds complexity that requires a separate product decision.
✕ Reporting dashboard or analytics — V1 queue view is a list, not a dashboard. Historical trend analysis follows once baseline data exists.
✕ Admin-configurable workflow rules — V1 is a single shared workflow. Making it configurable adds governance complexity not justified until baseline is proven.
"Not in scope" means three different things: (1) Decided out — explicitly excluded by choice with a reason. (2) Still undefined — may be in scope but rules not yet agreed. (3) Never discussed — not on anyone's radar. Only (1) is a true non-goal. (2) and (3) are planning blockers wearing the disguise of a scope decision.
4 Section 4 — PRD Risk & Clarification

Identifying Unknowns, Risks, and Open Questions

Scope is bounded. What is still undefined is no longer abstract — it is the specific set of things blocking estimation.

Core idea: An unknown → Risk → Clarification Question. Each undefined item must be named as an unknown, mapped to the planning damage it creates, and converted into a specific answerable question before estimation can begin.

Unknown → Risk → Clarification Question

Planning Blocker Framework — Support Escalation Workflow
Unknown — not decided
Who sets priority, and who can revise it
What fields are required in the submission
What each status actually means, and what moves issues between states
Engineering review outcomes — accept, return, defer
What resolution means — closing criteria
Risk — damage if ignored
Queue order distorted. Priority disputes before work even begins.
Incomplete issues enter review. Churn before triage, not during.
Three teams read each status differently. Visibility becomes misleading.
Decisions are untracked. No audit trail. Submitters don't know why issues were returned.
Issues closed inconsistently. QA cannot verify "done."
Clarification question — removes it
Who is authorized to assign or revise priority, at what stage, and what distinguishes High from Critical?
What minimum fields must exist before engineering is expected to begin review?
What does each status mean operationally, and what event moves an issue from one state to another?
What are the explicit outcomes of the review step, and what state does each outcome trigger?
What conditions must be true before an issue can be closed, and who is authorized to mark it resolved?

Seven planning blockers — these cannot be estimated around

  • ✕
    Required submission fields — intake form cannot be specified or built without knowing what is required
  • ✕
    Priority classification rules and ownership — classification mechanism cannot be specified or tested
  • ✕
    Status definitions and transition conditions — status tracking cannot be built or tested without state meaning
  • ✕
    Engineering review outcomes — the review decision point cannot be designed without knowing its outcomes
  • ✕
    Role permissions — write actions cannot be implemented without knowing who is allowed to perform them
  • ✕
    Resolution criteria — the closed state cannot be specified or tested without a definition of done
  • ✕
    Measurable success targets and baselines — without targets, no post-launch validation is possible
Clarification question rule: "Can you clarify priority?" is a bad question — it restates confusion. "Who is authorized to assign or revise priority, what criteria distinguish High from Critical, and does priority affect review order?" is a useful question — it removes planning ambiguity.
"An unknown is not yet a risk until you understand what damage it creates. A risk is not yet actionable until you can phrase the question that would remove it."
5 Section 5 — PRD to Engineering Structure

Breaking the PRD into Epics, Stories, and Tasks

The PRD is clear enough to begin structuring work. This is the engineering execution layer. Blockers do not disappear — they stay visible in every work item that depends on an unresolved decision.

Core idea: Epics come from scope decisions. Stories come from user needs. Tasks come from implementation work. A backlog becomes dangerous when implementation steps are written as if they were user stories.

Epic / Story / Task — the three levels

Epic

A capability area from the PRD

Groups related work into a meaningful chunk. Comes from a scope commitment, a user need, or a workflow behavior. Not a sprint container.

E.g. "Escalation Intake" — the full capability for structured issue submission
Story

A user- or behavior-centered unit of work

Describes what a specific user needs the system to do, or what workflow behavior must exist. Never an implementation step. If it sounds like a build task, it is probably a task.

"As a support agent, I need a structured form so engineering receives the context needed for review."
Task

The implementation work for a story

What engineering builds to deliver the story. Belongs to the story. Never a user-facing value statement. A task list is not a story.

Build form UI · Add validation · Store record · Return incomplete submission

Seven epics — from V1 scope

E1 — Escalation Intake

Structured submission format replacing ad-hoc channels. All downstream behavior depends on reliable intake.

E2 — Priority Classification

Shared model applied to each issue. Blocked until rules and ownership are confirmed.

E3 — Engineering Review

Accept / return / defer decision point. Gatekeeping before work enters the engineering queue.

E4 — Assignment & Ownership

One visible owner per accepted issue. Follows acceptance — not raw submission.

E5 — Status Tracking & Visibility

Issue state visible to support and CS. Blocked until state model is agreed.

E6 — Resolution Handling

Defined terminal state with logged timestamp. Blocked until resolution criteria agreed.

E7 — Queue Oversight

Manager list view — incoming, assigned, stalled. Depends on reliable upstream data.

"A backlog becomes dangerous when implementation steps are written as if they were user stories."
Spot the fake story: "Create escalation form UI" is a task. "Add status dropdown" is a task. "Build database table" is a task. None of these express user value or workflow behavior. Each belongs under a story as a task — not as the story itself.
6 Section 6 — Engineering Execution Layer

Writing Acceptance Criteria and Delivery Readiness Rules

Stories need acceptance criteria before they can be estimated, reviewed, and tested. AC is not a task list — it is observable, testable behavior.

Core idea: A story is not ready because it exists in the backlog. It is ready when the team can tell what acceptable looks like — and when different people reading it would still build the same thing.

Four distinctions — not interchangeable

Story

What the system must do for a user

User- or behavior-centered. Defines the need, not the solution. Written from the user's perspective. Does not describe how it is built.

Acceptance Criteria (AC)

What must be true for the story to be accepted

Observable, testable behavior — not tasks. If you can't write AC, the story is not ready. If AC sounds like build steps, rewrite it as behavior.

Task

The implementation work required

What engineering builds. Belongs to the story. Not a story itself. Tasks answer "how." AC answers "when is this done correctly."

Definition of Done (DoD)

The team-level completion standard

Applies across all stories. Reviewed, tested, merged, documented as agreed. Not story-specific. Sits above AC — it does not replace it.

"AC defines what success looks like for this story. DoD defines what completion looks like for the team."

Pre-estimation readiness levels

Ready for Discussion
  • Story is written and scoped
  • AC is drafted — even if incomplete
  • Blockers are named
  • Team can examine and improve it
Ready for Estimation
  • Story behavior is agreed
  • AC items are testable
  • No unresolved product blocker
  • Failure behavior is specified
  • Dependencies are visible
Ready for Execution
  • Estimated and accepted
  • All blockers resolved
  • Tasks broken out
  • DoD understood and agreed
  • No open rule or permission gap
Weak AC warning signs: "The form should work correctly" — untestable. "Build the validation layer" — a task, not a behavior. "The user should be able to submit" — says nothing about what happens when it fails or what state the system enters after success.
7 Section 7 — Engineering Execution Layer

Building the Initial Delivery Breakdown

Stories are readiness-checked. Now sequence the work — what must happen first, what depends on what, and what cannot move until a blocker is resolved.

Core idea: Delivery order is earned by dependency, not by preference. Placing a blocked item in Slice 3 rather than Slice 1 does not remove the blocker. It only delays the moment the blocker becomes visible.

Sequencing logic — what makes something come first

Foundational dependency

Later behavior cannot exist without earlier behavior. Review cannot be built before intake exists. State visibility cannot be trusted before the state model is agreed.

Usable progress

What creates a shippable, testable outcome on its own? An intake form skeleton produces something testable. A manager overview without queue data produces noise.

Blocker timing

A blocked story is blocked in every slice — not just the first one. Sequencing it later delays the moment the blocker becomes visible. It does not resolve the blocker.

Fake Phase 1

The most dangerous delivery plan looks complete — every story has a slice — but no single item in Slice 1 can be completed in a reviewable state because a blocker affects each one.

First delivery slices — support escalation workflow

Slice 1 — Intake Foundation

Structured submission form (partial) · Issue record creation (partial) · Incomplete submission handling (blocked — required fields TBD)

Slice 2 — Review & Decision

Engineering review action (partial) · Return for more info (partial) · Priority classification (blocked — rules TBD)

Slice 3 — Ownership & Visibility

Assignment with visible owner (partial) · CS state visibility (blocked — state model TBD)

Slice 4 — Resolution & Oversight

Resolved state handling (blocked — resolution criteria TBD) · Manager queue overview (partial)

"A delivery plan is not real just because everything has a place on it."
8 Section 8 — Final Section

Building the Planning-Ready Action Plan

Delivery order is not yet an action plan. The team needs both — but finishing the sequence is not the same as knowing who does what next and under what condition.

Core idea: Sequencing tells the team what comes first. An action plan tells the team what to do first — with a named owner and an explicit next move for every item.

Four action lanes

✓ Move Now
  • Intake form structure shell
  • Issue record creation behavior
  • Review decision flow framing (structure only)
✕ Resolve Before Move
  • Required submission fields
  • Priority rules and ownership
  • Status definitions and transitions
  • Role permissions
  • Review outcomes
  • Resolution criteria
→ Prepare Next
  • Assignment + visible owner design
  • CS state visibility structure
  • Priority display in queue
◌ Hold for Later
  • Resolved state implementation
  • Full role-based visibility enforcement
  • Manager queue view (stable data required first)

Next action ownership

Engineering

Acts without waiting

Build form structure shell · Define issue record creation · Draft review action model (structure only) · Data model stub

Product Clarification

Must decide before engineering proceeds

Required fields · Priority rules · Status definitions · Resolution criteria · Duplicate handling depth in V1

Joint Decision

Both must agree before build

Role permissions · Review outcomes · State visibility logic · Measurement baselines

Validation

Verify before moving forward

Story readiness check before estimation · AC verification once blockers resolve · Slice 2 pre-entry check after Slice 1 is stable

"A plan is not execution-ready just because the work is listed. It becomes execution-ready when next action, ownership, and blocker status are explicit."

Cheat sheet — the rules that matter most.
