Day 6 · Capstone Delivery

You built the work.
Now defend the story.

Day 6 is the final capstone day. The goal is not to learn a new tool. The goal is to package, demonstrate, explain, and defend the project you built across the program.

Carried forward from Days 1-5

Your capstone should connect the full journey: requirement framing, delivery planning, workflow structure, AI-assisted engineering, collaboration artifacts, debugging evidence, deliverable quality, and final demo readiness.

1 · What this day is about

Day 6 is not a loose presentation day.

This day is about proving that the project can be understood by someone who was not sitting beside you while you built it. A strong capstone is clear, evidence-backed, honest about limitations, and easy to review after the live session ends.

Core idea: A project is not complete because work was done. It is complete when the team can explain, demonstrate, defend, package, and carry the work forward.

Before Day 6

Artifacts exist

You have outputs from earlier days: plans, boards, code, tests, AI notes, reviews, fixes, and documentation.

During Day 6

Artifacts become a story

You connect the work into one delivery narrative: problem, decisions, implementation, evidence, limits, and next steps.

After Day 6

Project becomes reviewable

The package should be understandable, accessible, and useful for portfolio, interview, or evaluator review.

2 · Day journey

The capstone flow.

Day 6 is intentionally lighter on new content and heavier on demonstration, review, feedback, and final packaging.

Step 1
Final package check
Make sure repo, board, docs, tests, links, recordings, and evidence are organized and accessible.
Step 2
Demo protocol
Present the project as a delivery story, not a tool tour. Start with the problem and end with evidence and next steps.
Step 3
Team presentations
Run live demos or recorded walkthroughs with clear timing and visible explanation of what was built.
Step 4
Review and Q&A
Answer questions using artifacts and evidence, not vague confidence or defensive explanations.
Step 5
Portfolio bridge
Turn the project into a short professional story for portfolio, interview, or final evaluator review.
Step 6
Staying current
Choose habits for keeping tools, workflow assumptions, and project evidence up to date after the program.
3 · Core rules

Rules to remember before presenting.

These protect the demo from becoming only screen sharing or slide reading.

Story

Demo the delivery path

Show how the project moved from requirement to plan to implementation to evidence.

Evidence

Prove, do not only claim

Use tests, logs, screenshots, board state, PRs, docs, or workflow evidence.

Limits

Name limitations honestly

Incomplete work is not automatically failure if the limit, risk, and next action are clear.

Package

Make it reviewable later

Someone should understand the project after the session without needing you beside them.

Weak final demo
Starts with tool screens before explaining the problem.
Shows many pages but no decision trail.
Says “it works” without showing evidence.
Hides blockers, tradeoffs, or unfinished parts.
Leaves links private or files scattered.
Stronger final demo
Starts with the problem and project goal.
Shows the decisions that shaped the output.
Uses artifacts to prove correctness and readiness.
Names limits and next improvements professionally.
Submits a clean package a reviewer can open later.
4 · Section reference

Use this as your Day 6 checklist.

Open each block to review the capstone expectation, decision rule, and what to avoid.

1
Final capstone package
+

Your package is the reviewer’s path through your work after the live demo ends. It should not feel like a messy folder of unrelated outputs.

Minimum package contents
Project repo
Working code or implementation files, with clear structure and a readable README.
Docs
Setup steps, usage notes, project decisions, assumptions, limitations, and next actions.
Tests / evidence
Proof that the project was checked: tests, logs, screenshots, recording, manual verification, or review notes.
Board / plan
The planning layer that shows backlog, workflow, priorities, blockers, and delivery reasoning.
AI / debug appendix
Where relevant: prompt logs, AI verification, debugging notes, RCA, or rollback thinking.
Rule: the reviewer should not need to guess where the important evidence lives.
2
Demo protocol
+

A demo is not a tour of every file or screen. It is a short argument that your work is understandable, usable, and evidence-backed.

Recommended demo order
1 · Problem
What problem did the project solve or improve?
2 · Scope
What did you build, and what did you deliberately leave out?
3 · Decisions
Which technical, workflow, or product decisions shaped the project?
4 · Evidence
What proves the output is correct, usable, or honestly bounded?
5 · Next
What would you improve in the next sprint or next version?
Memory line: a strong demo has a beginning, a decision trail, evidence, and a next step.
3
Evidence map
+

Evidence is what makes the project easier to trust. Use evidence to support your claims instead of relying on confidence.

Planning evidence

Requirement framing, scope boundaries, non-goals, backlog, sprint plan, blockers, and workflow rules.

Build evidence

Repo structure, commits, PRs, code review notes, implemented features, and release notes.

Quality evidence

Tests, logs, screenshots, debugging notes, RCA, validation results, and known limitations.

Weak evidence
“We tested it.”
“The AI helped us.”
“The dashboard looks done.”
“The bug is fixed now.”
Stronger evidence
Test results or a reproducible check are shown.
AI output is paired with diff, review, or verification notes.
The dashboard answers a clear follow-up question.
The bug has repro steps, root cause, fix, and regression test.
4
Q&A and defense
+

Q&A is not an attack. It is a professional review moment. Answer by connecting your decision to evidence.

  • Start with the answer, not a long explanation.
  • Point to the artifact that proves or supports the answer.
  • Name uncertainty clearly when something is not fully solved.
  • Explain the next action if the reviewer finds a real gap.
Strong answer pattern
Claim
State the answer clearly.
Evidence
Show where the artifact, test, board, or note supports the answer.
Limit
Name what is still incomplete or risky, if relevant.
Next move
Explain what you would improve next.
5
Feedback and review
+

Feedback should make the next action clearer. Vague praise and vague criticism are both weak.

Weak feedback
“Good job.”
“The demo was confusing.”
“You need more details.”
Stronger feedback
“The repo setup is clear, but the README needs test commands.”
“The demo showed the feature, but not the validation evidence.”
“The board is useful, but blocked items need named reasons.”
Rule: useful feedback names the artifact, the issue, and the improvement.
6
Portfolio and interview story
+

Your capstone becomes more useful when you can explain it in 3-4 clear sentences without exaggerating.

Project story template
Problem
What workflow, product, or engineering problem did the project address?
Action
What did you build, organize, automate, test, or document?
Evidence
What proof shows the project is reviewable or usable?
Learning
What tradeoff, limitation, or next improvement can you explain professionally?
Avoid: “I used many tools.” Say what problem those tools helped you solve and what evidence shows the work is real.
7
Staying current after the program
+

Staying current does not mean chasing every new tool. It means knowing how to update your assumptions when tools, workflows, and best practices change.

  • Follow official docs, release notes, changelogs, and trusted product updates.
  • Re-test old tool workflows before teaching them, using them, or building work around them.
  • Keep a small learning log: what changed, why it matters, and what you will try next.
  • Update your portfolio package when the project improves or the evidence becomes stronger.
Memory line: professional growth is not only learning new tools; it is keeping your judgment current.
5 · Quick reference

Capstone cheat sheet.

Use this before submitting or presenting.

Problem: The audience understands what pain or workflow issue the project addresses.
Scope: The audience can tell what was included, excluded, delayed, or still unresolved.
Delivery path: The project connects back to requirement, plan, execution, build, review, and quality evidence.
Artifacts: Links, files, docs, screenshots, repo, board, and recordings are accessible and organized.
Evidence: Claims are supported by tests, checks, screenshots, logs, review notes, or demo behavior.
Limitations: Gaps and risks are named professionally with next actions.
Q&A: Answers connect claim → evidence → limitation → next move.
Portfolio: The project can be explained in a short professional story.

The final Day 6 move.

Do not only show that work exists. Show that the work can be understood, trusted, reviewed, and improved. That is what turns a class project into a professional delivery artifact.
